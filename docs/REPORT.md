# NBA Quant Lab: player box-score forecasting and game simulation

**Question.** Can a possession-level simulation forecast every player's box score - minutes, points, rebounds,
assists, threes, steals, blocks, turnovers - as calibrated, internally consistent probability distributions, and does
modelling players improve the forecast of the game itself?

**Design.** Six regular seasons (2020-21 .. 2025-26, 7,230 games) reconstructed from play-by-play. History seasons
2020-23 give priors and training data; every hyper-parameter, the simulator's calibration and the blend weight were
chosen on **2023-24** (tuning); **2024-25 and 2025-26** (test, 2,460 games) were scored once with everything frozen.
All forecasts are walk-forward: only games on earlier dates are used. Numbers below come from
[FINDINGS_DATA.md](FINDINGS_DATA.md), regenerated by `quantlab report`.

## Results in one table

Test seasons 2024-25 and 2025-26, every model scored on the same 52,865 player-games (conditional on the player
appearing) and 2,460 games. Lower is better everywhere except coverage.

| | Points MAE | Points CRPS | Points calibration error (PIT) | PRA 80% interval coverage | Spread RMSE | Total RMSE |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Season average | 4.667 | 3.387 | 0.246 | 72.4% | | |
| Last-10 average | 4.691 | 3.377 | 0.243 | 72.5% | | |
| Per-minute x projected minutes | 4.448 | 3.202 | 0.233 | 73.3% | | |
| Empirical-Bayes rates (closed form) | 4.406 | 3.146 | 0.221 | 73.8% | | |
| Gradient boosting (LightGBM) | **4.380** | 3.128 | 0.218 | 73.8% | | |
| Team-first reconciliation | 4.384 | **3.076** | 0.140 | 74.0% | 14.334* | 18.484* |
| **Possession simulation, bottom-up** | 4.412 | 3.084 | **0.038** | **79.7%** | 14.514 | 18.662 |
| Possession simulation, team-constrained | 4.410 | 3.085 | 0.043 | 79.5% | 14.322 | 18.658 |
| Team model (ratings) | | | | | 14.334 | 18.484 |
| **Blend: team model + simulation** | | | | | **14.292** | **18.474** |

\* the team-first reconciliation reproduces the team model's score distribution by construction.

**What this says.**

1. **Point forecasts: simpler models are as good.** For the *average* stat line, gradient boosting and closed-form
   empirical-Bayes rates are as accurate as the full simulation or slightly better (points MAE 4.380 vs 4.412, a 0.7%
   difference; rebounds within 0.02, steals, blocks and turnovers within 0.01). Every model-based approach beats season
   and last-10 averages by 5-6%; most of that gain comes from projected minutes (per-minute rates x projected minutes
   alone reach 4.448).
2. **Distributions: the simulation is the best calibrated.** Points and points+rebounds+assists - the statistics props
   are written on - have lumpy, skewed distributions (2s, 3s, free throws, minutes risk) that a negative binomial
   around a good point forecast cannot represent: their PIT histograms are badly off (calibration error 0.22) and
   80% intervals cover only 73-75%. The simulation's are close to flat (0.04) with 80% PRA coverage of 79.7%, and it
   has the best or tied-best CRPS for rebounds, PRA, steals and turnovers; the team-first reconciliation edges it on
   points CRPS (3.076 vs 3.084). Simulated over/under probabilities are reliable (see the Validation page).
3. **Team forecasts: player modelling helps spreads only in combination.** The bottom-up simulation alone is worse
   than the team model for spreads and totals (spread squared-error reduction -5.18, 95% CI -9.30 to -1.11).
   Blended with the team model (75/25, weight chosen on 2023-24), it improves spreads significantly - squared-error
   reduction +1.21 (95% CI +0.16 to +2.22), RMSE 14.334 -> 14.292, best CRPS - and leaves totals unchanged
   (+0.39, CI -0.78 to +1.55). The improvement is real but small: knowing who plays adds information that team
   ratings lack, while team ratings carry defence and system effects that box-score player rates miss.
4. **Architecture.** We keep the *hierarchical* design - the team model sets pace and the environment, the possession
   simulator allocates opportunities to lineups and players, and team scores are the sum of player scores - in its
   blended form. It is the only approach that is calibrated for points and PRA, internally consistent in every
   simulated game, supports injury and lineup scenarios mechanistically, and costs 68 µs per simulated game
   (20,000 games in 1.4 s). The team-first reconciliation is a strong, cheap alternative for point-level accuracy
   and is kept as a baseline; it cannot express lineup or rotation effects.


## 1. The data foundation is exact enough to model from

Player box scores derived from the play-by-play match NBA.com's official box scores: for 2023-24, minutes within one
second for 99.97% of player-games, points exactly for 99.98%, plus-minus 99.80%, every stat exactly for 99.27% (the
residual differences are symmetric post-game stat corrections the archived feed lacks). The reconstruction also yields
what box scores cannot: exact on-court team totals (true usage rate), live rebound chances, five-man stints and
possessions with timestamps. See [DATA.md](DATA.md).

## 2. Minutes: the hard part, and where most of the gain comes from

| Minutes model (test seasons, 52,950 player-games) | MAE | RMSE |
|---|---:|---:|
| Season average | 5.32 | 7.01 |
| Last 10 games | 5.20 | 6.86 |
| Decayed average | 5.03 | 6.62 |
| LightGBM, no lineup information | 4.43 | 5.76 |
| **LightGBM, starting lineups known** | **4.30** | **5.59** |
| Simulation (enforces 240 minutes, rotations, fouls, blowouts, overtime) | 4.31 | 5.60 |

* The gradient-boosting model is 14% better than a decayed average and 19% better than a season average, with the
  same gain in all three walk-forward seasons.
* **A leak we found and removed.** Using everyone who appeared in a game to describe teammate availability leaks the
  outcome: deep-bench players appear because a game became a blowout. Treating only rotation players' participation as
  known pregame (the injury report) and fringe players through their baseline minutes cost 0.07 minutes of MAE - the
  size of the leak.
* **Forcing 240 minutes per team did not help** a model that already sees teammate absences (MAE 4.37 vs 4.30); the
  simulator enforces the constraint instead, with five players on the floor at every moment.

## 3. Player rates and matchups

Empirical-Bayes rates with per-statistic decay and shrinkage reproduce known stability: three-point percentage needs
the most regression (half-life 250 days, prior worth 160 attempts), usage and shot profile the least (half-life 40
days). Opponent effects are real but small: dropping every opponent feature from the gradient-boosting player models
raises points MAE by 0.006 (95% CI 0.003 to 0.009) - about 0.1%. For turnovers the effect is not distinguishable
from zero.

## 4. Two empirical regularities the simulator needed

* **Usage is super-linear.** When the simulator shares a lineup's plays in proportion to usage^alpha, points MAE on
  tuning games was best at alpha = 1.3 (4.571 / 4.440 / 4.406 / 4.457 / 4.588 for alpha = 0.7 / 1.0 / 1.3 / 1.6 / 1.9):
  the offense runs through its highest-usage players more than proportionally.
* **The usage-efficiency tradeoff.** With teammate absences as a natural experiment (59,494 rotation player-games,
  2021-24), each +10% of relative usage costs a player 0.88 points of true shooting (SE 0.20). The naive regression
  has the opposite sign (+0.20 per +10%), because hot shooters both shoot more and shoot better. Without this
  tradeoff, removing a star *raised* his team's simulated scoring (his shots went to teammates at their usual
  efficiency); with it, losing Jaylen Brown costs Boston about 0.8 points and losing Nikola Jokic costs Denver about
  3.5 points and a 4.5-point margin swing.
* **Real scores are less variable than independent possessions imply.** Independent possessions give a team-points
  SD of about 12.8; a good pregame model's residual SD is about 11.5 *including* its own error. The simulator therefore
  has two small mean-reversion terms (efficiency drifts against a leading team and against a team running hot), and
  the team-level shooting "night" that seemed natural was calibrated to zero.

## 5. Why model the game jointly

Adding up independently projected players does not give a sensible team forecast (test seasons, 4,920 team-games):
summing gradient-boosting point projections misses the team model's expectation by 5.9 points on average and predicts
the actual team score worse than the team model does (RMSE 13.2 vs 11.7); treating players as independent implies a
team-score SD of 18.6 points against a realised 13.2 (80% intervals cover 93%). Teammates compete for the same
possessions, so their points are negatively correlated. In the simulation every team score is the sum of its players'
points, every player's points are 2·2PM + 3·3PM + FTM, minutes add to five times the game length, and assists never
exceed made field goals - asserted on every simulated game.

## Limitations

* **No live 2026-27 data.** The pinned mirrors end with 2025-26 and the NBA's CDN refused this environment's requests;
  the dashboard projects any historical date as "upcoming" (pregame information only) instead of tonight's slate.
* **Availability is taken from who appeared** for rotation players (a stand-in for the injury report and inactive
  list, which are public before tip-off but not in the sources). Late scratches and in-game injuries are therefore
  slightly easier here than in live use. Deep-bench participation is *not* assumed known; it is hard to predict
  (observed appearance rates span only about 33% to 51% across the predicted range) and fringe-player props should be
  treated with caution.
* **Defense beyond the box score is not modelled at the player level.** A player's defensive value enters through
  steals, blocks, rebounding and fouls, plus team-level style factors; removing an elite defender changes the
  opponent's shooting only through those channels.
* **Matchup-driven substitutions and coaching changes** are not modelled; rotations follow each player's role,
  minutes plan, fouls, score and time.
* **Single-season tuning.** The simulator's calibration constants were fitted on one season (2023-24) and held
  fixed; drifts in league style (rising three-point rate, rising offensive rebounding) are absorbed by the decayed
  league priors, not by re-tuning.
* **Prop probabilities are model probabilities**, without market prices; no claim is made about beating a sportsbook.
